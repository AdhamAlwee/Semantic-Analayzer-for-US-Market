\chapter{RESULTS AND DISCUSSION}

This chapter presents the results in the order of the experiments described in Chapter~3. The sentiment models are first compared on labelled text. The ablation study, the sentiment-model comparison on prediction and the news-source comparison are then reported, and the findings are discussed in the final section.

The class-weighted LSTM was used to test this explanation. Once the price-only network was no longer able to neglect a class, the gains fell to 0.000, 0.018 and 0.017, none of which was significant. This pattern was close to that of XGBoost, so the gains of the plain LSTM were treated as an artifact of class imbalance and not as evidence of predictive value.

Adding sentiment also did not improve the return forecasts. For XGBoost, the change in RMSE was negligible and not significant. For the class-weighted LSTM, the RMSE became significantly worse with sentiment at three days (+0.033, $q = 0.032$) and at five days (+0.110, $q = 0.018$).

The company-specific features did not add further value. In Table~\ref{tab:ablation-f1}, the third feature set generally scored at or below the second, except for the class-weighted LSTM at five days. Company features did produce a significant gain for WFC at three days in both LSTM variants, but this result was isolated and was not repeated for the other stocks.

The importance of the features in XGBoost was consistent with these findings. The average gain of the twenty features was almost uniform, ranging from 2.18 to 2.87, with the decayed sentiment score ranked fifth. No single feature, whether price or sentiment, dominated the predictions, which is the pattern expected when the targets contain little learnable signal.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The main finding was that FinBERT sentiment from news headlines did not significantly improve the prediction of stock movement beyond price and volume features. XGBoost and the class-weighted LSTM both led to this conclusion under corrected significance tests, and it was unchanged when the sentiment was lagged to remove possible look-ahead. All of the models also remained close to chance, and none improved on a zero-return forecast. These results are consistent with the efficient market hypothesis, according to which publicly available information is rapidly reflected in prices \parencite{fama1970efficient}. The news-source results point in the same direction: sentiment agreed with price moves around the publication day, but this agreement fell to the level of chance once that day was excluded. Sentiment appeared to describe price moves as they occurred, rather than to anticipate later ones.

This result does not prove that sentiment has no predictive value. The confidence intervals for XGBoost spanned approximately $\pm 0.013$ to $\pm 0.016$ around the estimates, so a gain smaller than about 0.013 in macro-F1 could not have been distinguished from zero with the available test data, and a small effect could have gone undetected. The 5-day XGBoost gain had a confidence interval that excluded zero before correction for multiple comparisons, but it was not significant after correction and did not survive lagging. The correct conclusion is therefore that no reliable gain was detected, and not that the gain is zero.

These findings were weaker than some of those reported in the literature reviewed in Chapter~2. A FinBERT-LSTM was reported to improve on price-only baselines \parencite{gu2024predicting}, and a modest gain from sentiment was reported for FNSPID \parencite{dong2024fnspid}. The small gains observed in this project were in the same direction but were not statistically reliable. The difference may reflect the matched baselines, walk-forward testing and corrected significance tests used here, although differences in the stocks, periods and models also prevent a direct comparison. The source dependence reported by \textcite{liu2025enhancing}, in which the accuracy of FinBERT ranged from 32\% to 76\% across five outlets, was also observed in this project when the publication day was included, with agreement of 53\% to 72\% on INTC. That study appears to have scored sentiment against labels derived from price returns, which is close to the market agreement measured here, although the return window was not stated. The results of this project suggest that such differences between outlets may largely reflect same-day price reactions, as agreement fell to about 50\% for both Nasdaq and Benzinga once the publication day was excluded.

The plain LSTM illustrates why a performance gain should be examined before it is accepted. Its significant result would have supported the opposite conclusion had the predictions not been examined by class. Macro-F1 rewarded the additional down predictions even though they were no more precise than the base rate. The class-weighted variant provided a controlled way to separate this effect from genuine information.

The sentiment-model comparison also showed that the choice of model cannot be settled from a text benchmark alone. VADER and BART-MNLI scored lowest on labelled text, but their lagged gains at three days were comparable with those of the finance-domain models. The ranking of the models also changed when the sentiment was lagged. As none of the gains was significant, accuracy on labelled text and usefulness for prediction could not be shown to be related in either direction. FinBERT remained a reasonable choice, given its balanced performance on labelled text and its low computational cost, but these results do not show that it is the best predictor.

Several limitations apply to these findings:
\begin{itemize}
\item FNSPID timestamps are date-only, so news could not be aligned with the market close, and the effect of news within the day could not be studied;
\item only headlines were used, and not full article text;
\item the outlets other than Nasdaq were available only until June 2020, and Nasdaq supplied 85.7\% of the headlines, so the source comparison was limited to a short period;
\item the five stocks were selected for their news coverage, so the results may not generalise to smaller or less covered stocks;
\item hyperparameters were fixed and not tuned, in order to keep the comparisons matched, so better-tuned models might perform differently; and
\item the LLM was limited to 1.5 billion parameters by the available hardware, so the results do not extend to larger LLMs.
\end{itemize}
